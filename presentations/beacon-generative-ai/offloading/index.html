What we offload, what we learn

Generative AI, study habits, and the difference between assisted performance and durable learning

Human-reviewed and approved for public release · Evidence checked 25 September 2026 · Funding and interests disclosed

EssayBibliographyProvenance appendixReview scopeAcknowledgements

Essay

Less work now is not the same as less ability later

Generative AI can help produce a good answer without ensuring that its user has learned how to produce one. It can also be used to organize practice and feedback. The evidence supports a conditional conclusion: outcomes depend on what the learner delegates, what the system asks the learner to do, and how learning is measured. Neither “AI makes us less capable” nor “AI makes us better learners” adequately describes the studies reviewed here. C1

Cognitive offloading means moving some processing or remembering into external actions or resources: notes, reminders, a calculator, or an AI assistant. Its immediate purpose is to reduce the demands a task places on internal cognition. That reduction is not itself evidence of deteriorating cognitive capacity. We need to distinguish assisted task performance, subsequent unaided recall or skill, and broad, lasting cognitive change. These are different outcomes, requiring different measurements. Risko and Gilbert; C2

Nor does the shared label make all aids interchangeable. Saving a reminder and delegating an explanation remove different activities from the learner’s work. The useful question is which operations remain available for practice: remembering a fact, constructing an argument, diagnosing an error, or evaluating a source. This is a design question as well as a question about individual habits. C1

Memory costs are real, but conditional

In a copying task, Grinschgl and colleagues found that greater offloading improved immediate performance while reducing later memory. Advance awareness of a memory test substantially reduced the memory cost in a forced-offloading condition. Conversely, Storm and Stone reported better memory for new information after earlier material had been saved. Offloading can change where limited effort goes; its consequences need not be uniformly harmful. Neither experiment measured GenAI use or a general decline in intelligence. Grinschgl et al.; Storm and Stone; C3

Retrieval practice offers a firmer basis for study design than familiarity with a fluent explanation. In Roediger and Karpicke’s experiments, repeated study helped on an immediate test, while prior recall testing produced better retention after delays. For AI-assisted study, this supports keeping opportunities to retrieve from memory. Applying that finding to a particular AI workflow is a reasoned design choice, not proof that the workflow works. Roediger and Karpicke; C4

The form of assistance changes the result

Bastani and colleagues randomized high-school mathematics classrooms to different forms of assistance. A relatively unrestricted GPT interface improved assisted practice, yet subsequent unassisted exam performance was about 17% lower relative to the control group—not a 17-percentage-point fall or a measure of general cognition. A tutor using teacher-informed hints and safeguards largely avoided that penalty, without a statistically significant exam advantage over control. This was a short-term result in one Turkish school. Bastani et al.; C5; N1

Positive results also matter. Kestin and colleagues found greater immediate learning gains with a carefully constructed, self-paced AI tutor than with an active-learning physics class. The comparison tests instructional packages, including pacing and setting; it does not isolate the effect of merely adding a chatbot. Read together, these trials show that particular designs can yield different outcomes. They do not establish that one prompt or product setting reliably preserves long-term learning. Kestin et al.; C6

A preliminary coding experiment by Shen and Tamkin similarly found poorer immediate unaided understanding after AI-assisted work on an unfamiliar library. Within the AI group, more concept-focused interactions accompanied better learning than heavy delegation. Those interaction patterns were observational: they may reflect differences among learners as well as differences in use. The randomized comparison and the exploratory pattern analysis should not be treated as equally strong causal evidence. Shen and Tamkin; C7

Experience is informative; the claim still needs a boundary

A person who feels more fluent after a period without AI has identified something worth investigating in their own practice. Someone who accomplishes more with AI has also observed a meaningful outcome. Neither observation alone tells us whether the same person would retain more, transfer knowledge better, or perform better without assistance under comparable conditions. Productivity and learning deserve separate records. C8

The same caution applies to research headlines. Lee and colleagues’ worker survey concerns self-reported effort and confidence, not experimentally measured deterioration in cognitive capacity. The studies selected here do not establish a universal or permanent loss of cognition from GenAI. They also do not settle the effects of years of dependence, or which learners benefit most from which designs. Those are open questions beyond this short, selective review. Lee et al.; C9

Design study around what the learner must retain

A defensible study routine begins by naming what should remain possible without the assistant. Try a problem or explanation first. Ask for a targeted hint or critique, then revise and explain the result in your own words. Check substantive feedback against course materials or other reliable sources. Finally, close the assistant and solve a new problem; return later to test recall. Keep the unaided results separate from the polished work produced with help. This is a proposed routine, not a validated universal prescription. C10

The routine should preserve relevant thinking without making difficulty an end in itself. A learner may reasonably delegate formatting or organization while retaining responsibility for the concept being studied. Which work is expendable depends on the goal: calculating by hand, interpreting a result and checking a model are different skills. Assistance should be judged against the intended skill and the learner’s actual performance. C10

Didactopus was built with this concern in mind: its documented aim is structured guidance, practice and evaluation rather than assignment outsourcing. That is a plausible design response, not demonstrated protection against learning loss. A useful evaluation would compare it with alternative study methods using delayed unaided recall and new problems, while recording prior knowledge, time spent and the assistance received. Responsible use includes testing whether the intended benefit occurs. Didactopus design record; C11

R2 · Grinschgl et al. · laboratory experiments

Grinschgl, Sandra; Papenmeier, Frank; Meyerhoff, Hauke S. (2021). Consequences of cognitive offloading: Boosting performance but diminishing memory. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology; 74(9), 1477–1496. DOI record.

Tests a copying task, offloading costs and subsequent memory. Advance knowledge of a memory test qualifies the performance–memory tradeoff. It is not evidence about lifelong cognition.

Access and version: Full text available and targeted methods, results and discussion inspected in PMC; published article.

Status check: PMC record and targeted title search checked on 2026-09-25; no correction or retraction located.

R3 · Storm & Stone · laboratory experiments

Storm, Benjamin C.; Stone, Sean M. (2015). Saving-enhanced memory: The benefits of saving on the learning and remembering of new information. Psychological Science; 26(2), 182–188. DOI record.

Provides a counterexample to uniformly harmful offloading: the benefit concerns new information learned after saving, not improved recall of the saved file.

Access and version: Publisher/society abstract and bibliographic record only. Full methods and effect estimates were not inspected; used for a narrow qualitative claim.

Status check: Issue year 2015; first online 2014. Targeted search found a withdrawal of a different electrophysiology paper, not this article. No notice for this article located.

Review scope and update policy

This is a selective narrative review for the BEACON discussion, current through 25 September 2026. It began with Grinschgl et al. and Bastani et al., then sought contrasting outcomes, retrieval-practice foundations, designed tutoring, coding-skill formation and the limits of self-report. English-language primary research and original project documentation were preferred. It is not an exhaustive search or meta-analysis.

Search routes included journal and author pages, PubMed/PMC, arXiv and CiteGeist. Searches combined cognitive offloading, memory, recall, generative AI, tutoring, skill formation and correction/retraction terms. Claim-source fit was checked separately from bibliographic identity. Source access limits are attached to each record; no missing full text is represented as fully reviewed.

CiteGeist’s initial broad evidence check was unresolved. DOI-only free-text verification also returned ambiguous and sometimes unrelated candidates; these were not accepted automatically. Title-and-author queries recovered the intended DOI for all seven research articles, but their automated status remained ambiguous. Each was resolved against the inspected primary record, not promoted on a tool score. The selected bibliography follows the inspected primary records, with software-assisted BibTeX validation. Tool scores are not used as confidence in scientific claims.

Additional candidates were screened without being promoted to evidence: a logic-puzzle study used simulated perfectly accurate assistance rather than a generative model (arXiv:2608.23543v1); broad engagement studies did not directly address retained skill; and the essay-writing EEG preprint (arXiv:2506.08872v2) and an earlier critique were not used to make neurological claims because their full methods and version relationship were not adequately reviewed here. Exclusion does not establish that those works are invalid.

Corrections and versions were checked through the inspected records and bounded title/DOI searches. The Bastani affiliation correction is included as R5. Update this essay when a source is corrected, a relevant preprint changes, or stronger evidence on delayed unaided performance becomes available. Change the affected claim record, rationale, timestamp and artifact hashes; retain prior versions in version control.

The provenance register, annotated references and release review are separate from the essay’s evidence claims. The source cache and raw tool results remain outside the public directory. This essay introduces no participant data and quotes no meeting attendee. No external figure is reproduced. A bounded similarity review and structural/browser checks accompany the draft; their limits are recorded in publication-integrity-review.json.
